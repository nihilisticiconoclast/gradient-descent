What the loss is for

The goal is a rule that predicts y from x. A rule here is just a line, and a line is just two numbers: a slope m and an intercept c. To judge a candidate line, run it over points where the true answer is already known and measure how far each prediction lands from it. Square each miss, so that misses above and below the line cannot cancel and large misses count for more, and average them. That average is the loss. Zero means every prediction was exact; the larger it is, the further off the rule was.

Because every pair (m, c) has its own loss, the loss is a surface over the plane of possible lines. That surface is the right-hand map: the horizontal axis is m, the vertical axis is c, and the contours are lines of equal loss, exactly as contours on a map are lines of equal height. Amber marks the high ground, where the line is badly wrong. The lowest point of the valley is the best line. The data never moves; only the position on this map does.

Read plainly: the c gradient is minus twice the average miss. If the line sits mostly above the points, the misses are negative, the gradient is positive, and the step pushes c down. The m gradient is the same thing with each miss weighted by its x, because tilting the line moves the far-right predictions most.

The minimum is where both partial derivatives are zero, the same condition as finding the lowest point of a curve by setting its derivative to zero. For a straight line that system can be solved exactly (it is ordinary least squares; the cross on the map marks its answer). Gradient descent never solves it. Reading the numbers

Epoch counts updates. With five points and every point used in each update, one epoch is one step. m and c are the current line. L is the mean squared residual in units of y²; its square root is roughly the size of a typical miss in units of y. In the equation rows, the bold figure is the result; everything before it is this epoch's values substituted into the fixed formula. The update rows give the parameters for the next epoch, so step forward once and they reappear as the new m and c.

On the valley map, the red route is every (m, c) the descent has visited, the dot is the current position, and the dark arrow is the direction of −∇L, straight downhill. Its length is not to scale.

The controls

Play runs the descent, one epoch at a time for the first twenty and then faster. +1 epoch advances exactly one update, the best way to follow a single cycle through all five rows. Reset returns to epoch zero. The slider jumps to any epoch. None of these change the outcome: the whole run is computed once when the page loads, and the controls only choose which moment of it to show.

What is idealised

The five points are invented, chosen so the starting line (flat, at c = 1.25) has a loss near 0.59 and the run behaves like a typical small notebook exercise. The learning rate is fixed at 0.01, and every update uses all the data (full-batch descent); real training usually samples a subset per step and often adapts the step size. After 500 epochs the route has not quite reached the cross. That is the honest result at this learning rate, not a bug: the valley is long and shallow along its floor, so progress there is slow. A larger η would get there sooner, and a much larger one would overshoot and diverge. Finally, this valley is a perfect bowl with a single bottom. That is special to straight-line least squares; the loss surfaces of neural networks have many valleys, and descent finds one of them.
